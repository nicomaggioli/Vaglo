IN DETAIL

The whole system, in plain words.

Six things happen between a notice appearing on SAM.gov and a package leaving your office. Here is each one, what it does for you, and where the edges are.

Everything below is built and in daily use

01 · Finding the work

It watches SAM.gov all night

Every federal opportunity is posted to SAM.gov, and there are thousands of them. Nobody at a small firm has the hours to read that, which is why good work goes by unnoticed.

Every night it searches SAM.gov for your set-aside, keeps only what matches your NAICS codes, and downloads the entire package for each one: the base solicitation, every attachment, amendments, drawings. One folder per opportunity, ready to read.

It also re-checks what you are already tracking. Due dates move. Notices get withdrawn. Attachments appear quietly a week later. Your list reflects what SAM.gov says this morning, not what it said the day you found it.

And it buries the ones already decided. If an agency has effectively picked its winner, that notice stops taking up room on your board. It will not make that call without two independent signals and a named recipient.

02 · Reading it

It reads the solicitation, then checks with you

A federal package is rarely one file. It is a base solicitation plus attachments plus amendments, in PDF and Word and Excel, and the amendments quietly overrule the base.

It reads all of it. First it works out what kind of job this is, because a design job, a construction management job and a general construction job should not be bid the same way. Then it pulls out what matters: the scope, the roles they require, how they intend to score you, the set-aside and size standard, the dates, the page limits, how it must be submitted, and who to send questions to.

Then it stops and shows you what it understood. Every line comes with the sentence from the solicitation that produced it. If it read something wrong you say so, and it makes you write down why. If it missed scope, you add it.

There is no AI in that checkpoint, deliberately. An AI-written summary of an AI's reading would hide the exact mistake the step exists to catch. Your sign-off is tied to that reading, so if the solicitation gets re-read, the sign-off goes stale rather than quietly standing.

If the AI fails, the run fails loudly and tells you. There is a working backup switched off by default, because a silent fallback turns a broken process into an invisible one.

03 · Scoring it

A good match, or an okay one

Every opportunity comes back with a percentage, and you can see all three things that produced it.

1

What it read

A score out of 100 from holding the solicitation up against your firm. Do you have the roles they want, does your past work match, where is it, do you hold the certifications, does the scope line up. It arrives with the reasoning written out.

2

Your own rules

Adjustments you write yourself, in plain English, from the dashboard. “Anything past the Mississippi, minus fifteen.” Every adjustment shows you which of your rules caused it.

3

What it learned from you

Once you have made enough real calls, it starts noticing your patterns by state, agency, fee size, set-aside and discipline. Until then it carries no weight at all. It cannot learn the wrong lesson from your first ten decisions, because for your first ten decisions it is not voting.

04 · Picking the team

You choose the projects and the people

This is the real work of a qualifications submission, and at most firms it gets done from memory, the week it is due.

It goes in the opposite order from how it is usually done. First it ranks your whole portfolio against this solicitation. A project that fails a threshold is marked rather than hidden, so you can see what was ruled out and why. Then the team comes out of the projects that ranked, because the strongest thing you can say about a person is that they did this exact work, on a job you are already putting forward.

Nothing is decided for you. You choose the projects, the personnel and the subconsultants. Every suggestion carries the reason it was made.

Same inputs, same list, every time. There is no AI in the ranking, so it does not change its mind between runs.

Measured against a human

On a live solicitation, the list it produced was set against the team the firm’s owner had picked by hand. Nine of the fifteen top picks matched exactly. Of the six that differed, four of the owner’s picks were already sitting in the alternates, and all three subcontractor firms came out the same.

It also refused two picks the old way had let through, one of them a design that was never built, sitting third in a submitted package.

05 · Building it

The SF330 itself

Every section, generated from your own records and laid out on the real form. This is the part nothing else in the category does.

Résumés

One per person, per role, written from your records. Anything the draft claims that is not in that person’s real history gets flagged.

Project sheets

Each project laid out to the form. Only a genuine match may claim direct relevance; sharing a discipline is not enough.

The matrix

Twenty people across ten projects is two hundred boxes, and it is the section firms most often get wrong by hand. Built straight from your records, no AI involved.

The narrative

Eight to fifteen pages, ordered to mirror how the agency said it will score you, with more room where they weighted heaviest.

Partner forms

A qualifications form for every teaming firm, yours included. One real package carried ten. Chasing partners two days out is a job nothing else touches.

Putting it together

Cover, cover letter, contents with dot leaders, bookmarks, tab dividers, footers. One real package on record: 61 pages, 20MB.

06 · Checking it

It reads the solicitation back to you

Packages get thrown out for reasons that have nothing to do with how good the firm is. A form nobody signed. An attachment nobody remembered. A page limit missed by two.

Before you submit, it goes back through the solicitation requirement by requirement and checks your package against every one. What you have met, it confirms. What you have missed, it flags, with the line from the solicitation sitting next to it. Fix it yourself, or tell it to.

Anything it could not source stays a visible blank, and a do-not-submit file holds the package shut until a person has resolved every one. On one real package that gate held back 62 blanks.

A last pass then scores the finished package the way the evaluation board is going to, against the factors the agency published.

The guardrails

What it refuses to do

On a document that binds your firm to the federal government, what it will not do matters as much as what it will.

It will not invent a fact only you know

Availability percentages are never generated. On a federal form that is a staffing commitment, not a guess. Missing facts become visible blanks and the package stays shut.

The AI cannot change your records

The parts that read your documents cannot write to them. Every change is ordinary code, and an AI-suggested correction passes a check, then a trial run, then you. The chat editor can only ever touch two fields.

It will not move a project between firms

If a partner uploads a project sheet resembling one already credited elsewhere, it goes to review and writes nothing. A partner’s fee can never land in your own fee column.

Straight about the limits

What you would be buying into

Every system has edges. Better you hear these from me on a webpage than find them in month two.

It runs on your machine, not in the cloud

No logins, no user accounts, no shared database, nothing leaving your building. That is good for control of your data, and wrong if what you wanted was a hosted product with a support desk behind it.

Windows today

Document conversion, service management and the default file paths all assume Windows. A Mac or Linux office is real work, not a checkbox.

It makes the federal qualifications package

That covers any firm chasing federal design work, and the finding half carries over to construction management. It does not write general commercial RFP responses.

What comes out follows what goes in

It is built to say I don’t know rather than guess, so a thin database gives you a package full of blanks. That is correct behaviour, and it is why the archive work at the start is not optional.

It has not been run by a big proposal team

One firm, one main user. There is real hardening in there for several people working at once, but several people editing the same proposal at once is untested.

There is no undo in the chat editor

It shows you before and after and asks before it acts, but it does not keep a history. To reverse a team swap, you run the opposite swap.

Why you can rely on it
